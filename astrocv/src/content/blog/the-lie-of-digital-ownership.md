# The Lie Of Digital Ownership

For years, we've been told that digital is the future. Movies, games, music, books, software have all migrated toward downloads and streaming. It's convenient, instant, and requires no physical space.

But convenience comes with a trade-off: you don't actually own what you buy.

The difference between owning something and merely having access to it has never been more important.

## Buying Digitals Isn't Ownership

When you purchase a Blu-ray, a CD, a cartridge, or a printed book, you own a physical copy. You can play it offline, lend it to a friend, sell it years later, or keep it on your shelf indefinitely.

Digital purchases often work differently.

Most online storefronts sell licenses rather than permanent ownership. Those licenses exist only as long as the store, your account, the publisher's agreements, and the platform's terms remain in place.

## Digital Libraries Shrinks Overnight

The internet is full of examples where purchased content disappeared.

Movies and television shows have vanished from streaming services because of licensing agreements. Digital storefronts have removed games after publishers lost rights or released remastered editions. Entire services—including Google Stadia, Microsoft Books, and the Wii Shop Channel—have shut down, leaving customers scrambling to preserve their purchases.

The digital library you've spent years building ultimately depends on companies continuing to operate the services that host it.

## Streaming Quality Isn't Good

Streaming has improved dramatically, but convenience still comes with compromises. Streaming quality can fluctuate depending on internet congestion, bandwidth limits, or service-side compression.

A disc always plays the version stored on it.

## Physical Media Preserves History

Digital platforms constantly evolve, as well as their content.

A physical copy captures a work as it existed at a specific moment in time.

That matters for historians, archivists, critics, collectors, and anyone who wants to experience creative works as they were originally released.

Without physical preservation, portions of our cultural history risk becoming inaccessible.

## The Hidden Costs

Subscriptions have become the dominant way to consume entertainment.

Individually, they seem inexpensive. Collectively, they can become far more expensive.

Consumers pay more while often owning less.

## Digital Should Complement Ownership

Digital distribution offers genuine advantages. Instant downloads, automatic updates, cloud saves and others.

The issue isn't that digital exists. The issue is replacing ownership with dependence.

Convenience should complement ownership, not eliminate it.
